# Sunk Cost Fallacy

## One-sentence explanation

> The sunk cost fallacy is the tendency to let irrecoverable past costs influence current decisions
> when only future costs and benefits should matter.

## Why this matters

Most bad decisions people stay inside are not decisions they would make again. They are decisions
they already made, and are now defending. The job they should have left two years ago, the failing
project nobody will cancel, the relationship that ended emotionally long before it ended
practically — the reasoning that keeps a person in place is usually some form of *I have already put
too much in to stop now*.

That sentence is an error, and it is worth being able to hear yourself say it.

## The idea

At any decision point you are choosing between futures. The past is not one of the options.

Suppose you must choose between continuing (A) and stopping (B), and you have already spent an amount
C that you cannot recover under either choice. Then C appears in both branches:

```text
value(A) = C_spent + future_costs(A) + future_benefits(A)
value(B) = C_spent + future_costs(B) + future_benefits(B)
```

`C_spent` is identical on both lines. A quantity that is the same in every option cannot distinguish
between them. It cancels. Whatever makes A better or worse than B lies entirely in the future terms —
so the only decision-relevant question is *from here, which path is better?*, never *how much have I
already put in?*

The normative principle is simple enough to state in one line. The interesting part is that people
who fully understand it still violate it, including the people who teach it.

## Mechanism

There are four live explanations. They are not mutually exclusive, and **which one dominates is
genuinely unresolved** — a fact usually omitted from popular treatments.

**1. Waste avoidance.** [[Arkes & Blumer 1985 - The Psychology of Sunk Cost]] proposed that we are
enforcing a learned social norm — *do not be wasteful* — that is usually excellent advice and
misfires here. Abandoning something you paid for *feels* like waste, even though the waste already
happened.

**2. Self-justification.** Stopping is a public admission that the original decision was wrong.
Continuing defers that admission. This predicts the effect should be stronger when *you* made the
original choice than when you inherited it — and escalation is indeed sensitive to responsibility and
accountability ([[Sleesman et al 2012 - Cleaning Up the Big Muddy]]).

**3. Loss aversion and mental accounting.**
[[Thaler 1980 - Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice]] frames it as closing a mental account.
While a project is alive, the loss is unrealised. Cancelling books it. Continuing is a way of not
looking at the number.

**4. Reputation.** In repeated dealings, being known as someone who finishes what they start has real
value. Some apparent sunk cost behaviour is a rational investment in that reputation, which makes it
not a fallacy at all — see *Counterexample*.

These make **different predictions**. Waste avoidance predicts the effect with nobody watching and no
ego at stake. Self-justification predicts it mostly when you own the original decision. Designs that
cleanly separate them are rarer than the size of the literature suggests.

One clue constrains all four: **children and non-human animals appear to commit this error *less*
than adult humans do** ([[Arkes & Ayton 1999 - The Sunk Cost and Concorde Effects]]). That is the
wrong direction for a primitive cognitive limitation, and points toward an acquired rule applied too
broadly. We are not failing to learn something. We learned something useful and over-applied it.

## Example

**The theatre season tickets.** Arkes and Blumer sold season tickets at Ohio University's theatre and
randomly assigned buyers to pay full price ($15), $2 off, or $7 off. Everyone then held an identical
ticket to identical plays. Attendance in the first half of the season tracked the price paid: full
price ≈ 4.11 plays, discounted groups ≈ 3.3.

The randomisation is what makes this clean. The groups did not differ in how much they liked theatre;
the assignment saw to that. The only difference was money already gone and unrecoverable. It changed
behaviour anyway.

**Concorde.** Britain and France continued funding a supersonic airliner both governments had
internal analysis showing would never repay its development costs. The project is the eponym for the
effect in the animal-behaviour literature — the *Concorde fallacy* — because the argument for
continuing kept being the amount already spent.

**The everyday version.** Finishing a bad book because you bought it. Sitting through the second half
of a film you are not enjoying. Eating past full because the portion was expensive. The stakes are
trivial, which is what makes them good practice: the mechanism is identical to the version that costs
you a decade.

## Counterexample

Persisting because of past investment is **not always** an error, and the cases where it is rational
are the ones popular explanations get wrong.

- **The cost carries information.** Three years into a PhD, those three years are evidence — about
  your aptitude, your tolerance for the work, your fit. Updating on that evidence is correct
  Bayesian practice, not a fallacy. The distinction that matters: it is legitimate to be moved by
  *what the investment taught you*, never by *the investment itself*.
- **The cost changed the marginal structure.** If the expensive part is done and what remains is
  cheap, continuing may now be correct — because the *future* costs fell, not because the past ones
  were large. Same conclusion, entirely different reason.
- **Commitment has value.** If you renegotiate every commitment at every moment, present bias wins
  every time. Precommitting and then honouring it is a device against your future self, and its value
  depends on not treating each moment as a blank slate.
- **Reputation is a real future asset.** Where others observe whether you finish things, finishing has
  forward-looking value. The cost is not sunk if abandoning it damages something you still hold.

The test: **strike out the past spending and re-argue the case. If the argument survives, it was
never sunk cost reasoning.** If the only remaining argument is the spending itself, it was.

## Common misconceptions

**"Sunk cost means you should quit."** No. It means the money already spent is not a reason to
continue. Continuing may be entirely correct on forward-looking grounds. Reflexive quitting is not
the corrective — it is the same error with the sign flipped, and it is more fashionable.

**"It's about money."** Time, effort, and emotional investment all produce it. The specific claim that
sunk *time* matters less than sunk *money* is weaker than commonly asserted: a preregistered
replication found the money-over-time asymmetry in one study, both effects in another, and nothing at
all in a third ([[Petrov et al 2023 - Sunk Cost Effects for Time Versus Money]]).

**"Intelligent people are immune."** Cognitive reflection predicts susceptibility, but does not
eliminate it, and it is *crystallised* intelligence — accumulated knowledge and experience — that
predicts sunk cost behaviour, not fluid reasoning ability
([[Ronayne Sgroi & Tuckwell 2021 - Evaluating the Sunk Cost Effect]]). Knowing about the bias is not
the same as being protected from it.

**"It's a primitive instinct."** The comparative evidence points the other way: less of it in
children, no unambiguous cases in animals. It looks acquired.

## What this does NOT imply

- That persistence is a vice. Most things worth doing require continuing past the point where it
  stopped being fun. The fallacy is a claim about *which reasons* are valid, not about how long to
  keep going.
- That you should override the feeling. The discomfort of abandoning something is real information
  about what you value; it is simply not information about whether continuing is the better forward
  choice.
- That organisations escalate because their people are irrational. Frequently, continuing is
  individually rational for whoever would have to announce the cancellation — their incentives and
  the institution's diverge. That is an [[Incentives]] problem wearing a bias costume, and treating
  it as a training problem will not fix it.
- That the effect is well enough measured to tell you *how susceptible you personally are*. See
  below.

## Evidence

Claims are marked per [[Source Evaluation]].

| Claim | Marking | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Irrecoverable past costs change subsequent behaviour | **Well supported** | Field and lab evidence since [[Arkes & Blumer 1985 - The Psychology of Sunk Cost]]; meta-analytic means around d ≈ 0.43 for sunk cost effects |
| The effect survives real incentives, not just hypothetical vignettes | **Well supported** | 23% chose a strictly dominated lottery after earning it through real effort ([[Ronayne Sgroi & Tuckwell 2021 - Evaluating the Sunk Cost Effect]]) |
| Effect sizes shrink substantially under preregistered replication | **Well supported** | ϕc .61 → .38 in a close replication ([[Petrov et al 2023 - Sunk Cost Effects for Time Versus Money]]) |
| Standard vignette measures have poor internal consistency (ω = 0.14–0.57) | **Established** *(for those instruments)* | [[Białek & Biesiada 2025 - On the Low Reliability of Sunk Cost Vignettes]] |
| Which mechanism dominates — waste avoidance, self-justification, loss aversion, reputation | **Contested** | Competing accounts fit the same data; [[Sleesman et al 2012 - Cleaning Up the Big Muddy]] compares theories rather than settling them |
| Sunk time matters less than sunk money | **Contested** | Mixed across three studies in one preregistered replication ([[Petrov et al 2023 - Sunk Cost Effects for Time Versus Money]]) |
| Absent in non-human animals, weaker in young children | **Plausible** | [[Arkes & Ayton 1999 - The Sunk Cost and Concorde Effects]] — a strong negative claim from a 1999 review of a contested literature |
| A stable individual trait of "sunk cost susceptibility" exists | **Contested** | Low scale reliability undercuts it; [[Ronayne Sgroi & Tuckwell 2021 - Evaluating the Sunk Cost Effect]] finds individual predictors anyway |

## Limitations and uncertainty

The honest summary: **the aggregate effect is solid; the trait is not.**

Sunk cost is among the most cited findings in behavioural science and among the more poorly measured.
Decades of vignette research were never systematically validated, and when someone finally checked,
internal consistency ran as low as ω = 0.14 — responses are largely scenario-specific
([[Białek & Biesiada 2025 - On the Low Reliability of Sunk Cost Vignettes]]). This does not threaten
the experimental demonstrations, which are within-subject or randomised and do not depend on scale
reliability. It does threaten any claim of the form "this person is high in sunk cost bias," and a
good deal of the individual-differences literature built on those instruments.

Also unresolved, and worth holding loosely:

- **Mechanism.** Four accounts, all partially supported, rarely separated by design.
- **Field generality.** Most evidence is scenarios and lab tasks. The strongest real-stakes evidence
  is a single lottery-switch paradigm.
- **Where the line falls.** The rule *ignore sunk costs* is clean in the abstract and hard in
  practice, because real prior investment usually does carry genuine information about value. Most
  practical difficulty is in distinguishing the information from the attachment, and no study tells
  you where that line sits in your own case.
- **Provenance.** These sources were verified for citation and headline findings; the full texts were
  not read. Effect sizes quoted here should be checked against the papers before being published to a
  channel where they cannot be corrected.

### YouTube
**Hook:** There is a sentence that has cost people more years of their lives than almost any other:
*I've already put too much into this to stop now.*

**Core explanation:** You are always choosing between futures. Money already spent appears on both
sides of the comparison, so it cancels. It cannot tell you which option is better, because it is the
same in both.

**Example:** Ohio University theatre tickets — randomly assigned discounts, identical plays, and the
people who paid full price showed up more. The only difference was money already gone.

**Twist:** Children do this less than adults. So do animals. This isn't a primitive instinct we failed
to outgrow — it's a good rule, *don't be wasteful*, that we learned and then applied where it doesn't
belong.

**Takeaway:** Cross out what you already spent and make the argument again. If it still holds, stay.
If the only thing left is the spending, you have your answer.
