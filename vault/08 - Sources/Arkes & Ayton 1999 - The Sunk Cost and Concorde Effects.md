# Arkes & Ayton 1999 - The Sunk Cost and Concorde Effects

*Psychological Bulletin*, 125(5), 591–600. Subtitled "Are humans less rational than lower animals?"

## What it supports

The developmental and comparative evidence that makes sunk cost strange: the organisms we expect to
be *worse* at reasoning commit this error *less*.

## Key claims

- There are **no unambiguous instances of the Concorde fallacy in non-human animals**. Cases
  previously offered as examples admit forward-looking explanations.
- **Young children behave more normatively than adults** in economically analogous situations.
- This generates the central puzzle: why would a bias appear only in cognitively sophisticated adult
  humans?
- The authors' answer favours overgeneralisation of a learned rule ("do not waste") — which requires
  acquiring the rule in the first place, hence its absence in children and animals.

## Caveats

- A review from 1999; the animal literature has moved on and the strong negative claim
  ("no unambiguous instances") is the authors' reading of a contested literature.
- "Children are more rational here" is a claim about a narrow class of tasks, not a general finding
  about children's decision-making.
- Supports the *learned-norm* mechanism but does not exclude self-justification or loss aversion.
